# Live Introductory Workshop — Presentation Script

This script accompanies the **Automation Legends AI Learning Hub** curriculum and onboarding presentation. It is designed for a 35-minute guided presentation followed by up to 10 minutes of questions. The slides are available as the curriculum-and-onboarding deck; this document gives the facilitator spoken notes, audience interactions, and transitions.

> **Facilitator outcome:** New members leave knowing where to start, how to practice safely, and how they can contribute to the Week 4 capstone at their current skill level.

## Facilitation plan

| Segment | Timing | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Slides 1–3 | 0:00–0:09 | Welcome members and help them identify a learning path |
| Slides 4–5 | 0:09–0:16 | Make the onboarding sequence and learning loop concrete |
| Slides 6–8 | 0:16–0:25 | Explain beginner, intermediate, and advanced progression |
| Slides 9–10 | 0:25–0:32 | Invite members into the capstone and ongoing community support |
| Slide 11 and questions | 0:32–0:45 | Secure a clear next action and answer questions |

## Before the workshop

- [ ] Open the [AI Learning Hub](https://github.com/Automation-Legends/ai-learning-hub) in a browser.
- [ ] Open the [Week 4 capstone](week-04-collaborative-capstone.md), [grading rubric](week-04-capstone-feedback-and-grading-rubric.md), and [new-member onboarding guide](new-member-onboarding.md) in separate tabs.
- [ ] Enable captions and assign a chat moderator if possible.
- [ ] Prepare a poll: **What would you like AI to make easier this month?**
- [ ] Prepare the chat prompt: **This week, I will ________.**
- [ ] Use fictional, anonymized, or authorized public examples only. Do not request private documents, account details, or credentials from participants.

# Slide-by-slide script

## Slide 1 — Automation Legends AI Learning Hub

**Timing:** 0:00–0:03

**Purpose:** Welcome participants and establish a practical, responsible learning culture.

**Say:**

> Welcome to Automation Legends and the AI Learning Hub. This is a community for people who want to learn AI by building useful work, not by collecting tools or chasing every new feature.
>
> You do not need to be technical to start, and you do not need a polished project idea today. Our shared goal is simpler: choose one real, low-risk task; test an AI-assisted approach; verify what matters; and share what you learn safely.
>
> Throughout today’s workshop, please keep private documents, client information, passwords, and API keys out of chat. We will work with fictional examples so everyone can participate comfortably.

**Invite participation:**

> In the chat, share your name or preferred name and one word for what brought you here: prompts, learning, workflow, automation, project, or community.

**Transition:**

> The hub exists because AI becomes more useful when practice has a purpose.

## Slide 2 — AI learning works best when practice has a purpose

**Timing:** 0:03–0:06

**Purpose:** Frame the learning philosophy.

**Say:**

> It is easy to feel that AI learning means knowing every tool. It does not. In this community, we start with one real task and build from there.
>
> A useful practice cycle has four parts. First, choose a task that is low-risk and meaningful. Second, turn it into a clear prompt or workflow. Third, check the output before using it. Finally, share a safe reflection so the next member can learn faster.
>
> This matters because AI can generate a convincing draft even when details are missing or wrong. The human remains responsible for facts, decisions, privacy, and the final work.

**Invite participation:**

> Think of one task you repeat each week. It might be organizing notes, preparing a study plan, drafting a community post, or summarizing approved material. Keep that task in mind as we move through the paths.

**Transition:**

> The hub gives you a path that fits where you are today.

## Slide 3 — Three paths support every stage of growth

**Timing:** 0:06–0:09

**Purpose:** Help participants select a starting point without status pressure.

**Say:**

> The Learning Hub has three paths. These are not labels of status. They are starting points.
>
> Choose the Beginner Path if you want stronger foundations in prompting, verification, and a first project. Choose Intermediate if you already use AI and want to make your work more repeatable with workflows, automation, APIs, and testing. Choose Advanced after you can define boundaries and test a workflow; that path focuses on custom agents, approved knowledge retrieval, and evidence-grounded systems.
>
> If you are unsure, start with Beginner. Strong foundations make every later tool and workflow easier to evaluate.

**Run the poll:**

> Please choose the learning priority that best fits you today: better prompts, learning and research, routine tasks, content creation, understanding tools, or automation and projects.

**Transition:**

> Once you know your starting point, the first week should feel clear rather than overwhelming.

## Slide 4 — Onboarding makes the first week obvious

**Timing:** 0:09–0:13

**Purpose:** Convert interest into an attainable first-week plan.

**Say:**

> Here is the complete first-week journey. Choose one task. Learn the basics of privacy, verification, and accountability. Improve one prompt by adding a goal, context, rules, and output format. Verify one output before using it. Then share a safe reflection and ask for feedback.
>
> Notice what is not on this list: you do not need to automate everything, buy a tool, or build a complex system. The most valuable first week is one in which you discover a clear use case and a reliable review habit.

**Live micro-activity:**

> Write this sentence in the chat: “If AI could help me with one task this week, it would be ________.” Do not include private details. We will use a few examples to point people toward a starting resource.

**Transition:**

> The first-week sequence works because every path uses the same learning loop.

## Slide 5 — Every member follows the same learning loop

**Timing:** 0:13–0:16

**Purpose:** Explain the community’s practical learning method.

**Say:**

> The cycle is Learn, Practice, Verify, and Share.
>
> Learn means one practical concept at a time. Practice means applying it to a real, low-risk task. Verify means checking sources, calculations, missing information, and limits. Share means posting a sanitized reflection so others can reuse the insight.
>
> If something fails, that is useful data. The goal is not to make AI look impressive. The goal is to learn what makes a process more useful, safe, and reviewable.

**Ask:**

> Which part of this loop do you think is easiest to skip: learning, practice, verification, or sharing? Add one word in chat.

**Transition:**

> The Beginner Path makes the first two loops especially approachable.

## Slide 6 — Beginner members build confident AI habits

**Timing:** 0:16–0:19

**Purpose:** Explain the first four learning experiences and reduce intimidation.

**Say:**

> Beginner members start with four habits. AI Foundations helps you understand what generative AI can and cannot do. Prompting Essentials gives you a reusable structure for goals, context, constraints, and formats. Verify Before You Trust introduces the SCOPE check: source, calculation, omission, provenance, and escalation. The first AI project turns one recurring task into a reviewable workflow.
>
> A beginner contribution to this community can be very practical: improve a prompt, identify a missing detail, create a safe test case, write clearer instructions, or explain a workflow in plain language.

**Demonstrate briefly:**

> A vague request is: “Help me organize my week.” A clearer request states the approved notes, the desired table, the rule not to invent deadlines, and a request to flag missing information. The improvement is not magic; it makes the output easier to check.

**Transition:**

> Once members can form and verify a good request, they can build repeatable workflows.

## Slide 7 — Intermediate builders turn prompts into reliable workflows

**Timing:** 0:19–0:22

**Purpose:** Show how intermediate work remains bounded and human-centered.

**Say:**

> Intermediate work begins with a workflow map, not a tool. We define the trigger, the smallest authorized input, the AI-assisted transformation, the structured output, the reviewer, the destination, and the fallback.
>
> The aim is to create a useful draft for someone to review. We keep credentials secret, reduce permissions, test unusual cases, and define what happens when inputs are missing or uncertain.
>
> An intermediate builder does not need to create a fully autonomous system. A strong workflow might simply turn an approved form submission into a structured review draft and route it to a moderator.

**Ask:**

> In the task you chose earlier, where should a person still review the result before anything happens?

**Transition:**

> Advanced work builds on these boundaries rather than removing them.

## Slide 8 — Advanced members build bounded, evidence-grounded systems

**Timing:** 0:22–0:25

**Purpose:** Position agents and retrieval as governed systems, not magical autonomy.

**Say:**

> The Advanced Path introduces custom agents and vector databases. The key idea is not “make the system autonomous.” The key idea is “make the system bounded, observable, and evidence-grounded.”
>
> Advanced members write an agent contract: its purpose, allowed tools, approved knowledge, disallowed actions, output format, escalation rules, and owner. When a system retrieves from a knowledge base, the source needs to be authorized, current, and visible in the answer.
>
> A good first advanced project is read-only. It can recommend resources, summarize approved material, or prepare a cited draft. It should not publish content, use private data, or make high-impact decisions without a defined review process.

**Transition:**

> Week 4 brings these levels together in one project where every role matters.

## Slide 9 — Week 4 turns individual learning into a real community tool

**Timing:** 0:25–0:30

**Purpose:** Introduce the collaborative capstone and invite roles.

**Say:**

> The Week 4 capstone is the Community Resource Review Assistant. Its job is intentionally narrow: when someone suggests a public AI-learning resource, the workflow prepares a structured draft for a moderator. It can suggest tags, identify questions to verify, and flag concerns. It does not publish content or approve the resource.
>
> Beginners can define what makes a resource useful, design the review rubric, improve the prompt, test safe examples, and document the experience. Intermediate builders can map the trigger, design the structured output, set up the review queue, define a safe integration boundary, and test exception handling. Moderators remain accountable for every approval.
>
> The capstone is successful when the team produces a clear project contract, a workflow canvas, a review-safe prompt, five test cases, a documented improvement, and an honest limitation. A live deployment is optional.

**Invite participation:**

> In the chat, choose one role you would like to try: community researcher, prompt designer, quality reviewer, documentarian, workflow builder, evaluator, or moderator owner. You can choose a role you already know or one you want to practice.

**Direct participants to resources:**

> The capstone module and the feedback rubric are linked in the hub. The rubric rewards safe evidence and collaboration—not technical complexity.

**Transition:**

> You will not be working alone. The community provides structures that make progress visible.

## Slide 10 — Community support makes progress visible

**Timing:** 0:30–0:32

**Purpose:** Explain support systems, badges, and feedback.

**Say:**

> The community supports progress through workshops, discussions, templates, tool guides, badges, and project showcases. Use these resources as scaffolding, not as a checklist to rush through.
>
> The Week 1 Explorer and Week 2 Workflow Builder badges recognize evidence-based learning. The capstone rubric adds a fair way to review collaboration across beginner and intermediate roles. A strong project showcase includes what you tested, what you verified, what you changed, and what you would improve next.
>
> Asking a clear question, sharing a safe failure, or helping another member understand a boundary are all meaningful contributions.

**Transition:**

> Let’s turn this workshop into one specific next action.

## Slide 11 — Start small. Verify what matters. Build together.

**Timing:** 0:32–0:35, then Q&A through 0:45

**Purpose:** Secure a commitment and close with the community standard.

**Say:**

> Choose one task for this week, complete one learning activity, and share one safe reflection. That is enough to begin.
>
> If you are new, begin with AI Foundations or Prompting Essentials. If you have experience with workflows, read the Week 2 automation module and choose one human-review point. If you are ready to collaborate, open the Week 4 capstone and select a role.
>
> Start small. Verify what matters. Build together. The hub is public at github.com/Automation-Legends/ai-learning-hub.

**Commitment prompt:**

```text
This week, I will ________.
```

Invite participants to post one sentence in chat. Thank participants for their commitments.

## Question-and-answer facilitation

For the final ten minutes, use these response patterns.

| Question type | Facilitation response |
|---|---|
| **“Which tool should I use?”** | Ask about the task, data sensitivity, desired output, and review step before suggesting a tool category or hub resource. |
| **“Can AI do this automatically?”** | Ask what the final action is, who is affected, what data is involved, and where human approval should remain. Start with a draft-and-review workflow. |
| **“I am a beginner. Can I join the capstone?”** | Yes. Explain the beginner roles: learner research, prompt testing, quality review, documentation, and reflection. |
| **“What if my test fails?”** | Treat the failure as evidence. Record it, narrow the scope, add a check, and explain the next safe revision. |
| **“Can I share my work?”** | Yes, if it is sanitized, authorized, and honest about limitations. Use the Project Showcase template and contribution guide. |
